When I first moved into IT support, a lot of the job was fairly straightforward in my head.

Something was broken.

Find out why.

Fix it.

Move on to the next one.

That's still a big part of what I do.

But my thinking has changed a bit.

## The immediate problem still matters

If someone's laptop isn't working, they need their laptop working.

If they can't access something they need for work, that needs sorting.

If Outlook has stopped behaving, the user doesn't particularly care about my thoughts on service management.

They want Outlook working.

That's the reality of support.

Restoring service matters.

But I've started thinking about what happens after that.

## What if it keeps happening?

This is where things get more interesting.

If I fix the same problem once, that's useful.

If I fix it three times, I start wondering whether there is something else going on.

Maybe the underlying technical issue hasn't been addressed.

Maybe the process is causing it.

Maybe the documentation is unclear.

Maybe the same mistake keeps being made.

Maybe there is a dependency nobody has documented.

There are plenty of possibilities.

The point is to notice the pattern.

## Documentation was one of the first things that changed my thinking

I've worked on documentation where the written process didn't match what was actually happening.

That seems like a small problem.

But if someone follows an outdated process, they can create another problem.

So you fix the immediate issue.

Then you fix the information around it.

That's a different kind of improvement.

The first helps the person in front of you.

The second can help the next person too.

## ITIL helped put some structure around this

Studying ITIL has given me a better way of thinking about these situations.

There is a place for restoring service.

There is also a place for looking at recurring problems and improving the service.

I don't need to turn every ticket into a formal problem-management exercise.

Sometimes noticing something and raising it is enough.

But the mindset is useful.

Don't just look at the individual event.

Look at the pattern.

## I've started seeing the service around the ticket

This has probably been the biggest change.

A ticket doesn't exist on its own.

There is a person raising it.

There is a process behind it.

There might be a team that owns the service.

There might be another team that owns part of the technology.

There are systems, information, policies and dependencies involved.

The technical issue is only one part of the picture.

Once you start looking at that, some problems become easier to understand.

## The same thinking is behind my interest in ISO 27001

This is also why information security interests me.

Security isn't just about buying a tool or enabling a setting.

There is a wider picture.

What are the risks?

What are the controls?

Who is responsible?

What does the process say?

What actually happens?

What evidence do we have?

Where are the gaps?

That way of thinking feels like a natural extension of how I've started approaching support problems.

## I don't think every problem needs a big answer

This is important.

Sometimes the answer really is:

> Fix the thing and move on.

Not everything needs a process review.

Not every repeat issue needs a project.

Not every documentation gap needs a new system.

Sometimes you just need to update a page or tell someone what happened.

The point isn't to make everything bigger.

It's to make the right improvement when there is one.

## That's probably where I am now

I'm still a technical IT support engineer.

I still troubleshoot devices.

I still investigate Microsoft 365 issues.

I still deal with printers, access problems, applications and all the other things that turn up during a normal week.

But I'm also becoming more interested in what sits around those problems.

Why does this keep happening?

Could we make this clearer?

Is the process actually helping?

What information are we missing?

Could the service work better?

That's a change in how I think about the job.

I'm not moving away from the practical side of IT.

I'm building on it.

And for me, that's probably the most useful thing I've taken from the last few years.
