I get asked fairly often how someone goes from 13 years in the British Army into IT, usually with the assumption that the two have nothing in common. I understand why it looks that way from the outside. But a lot of what I do day to day, and a lot of where I want my career to go, traces straight back to things I learned in uniform long before I touched a service desk.

## Process, not discipline

The obvious one people expect me to say is discipline, and sure, that's in there. But it's not the interesting bit. The interesting bit is process.

The Army runs on process because the alternative, in a lot of situations, is genuinely dangerous. Every task has a standard way of being done, that standard exists because someone worked out the safest and most reliable way to do it, and you don't skip steps because you're in a hurry. That sounds rigid when I write it down, but what it actually builds is a habit of asking "is there a better, more consistent way to do this" rather than just doing the thing in front of you and moving on. That habit transferred almost directly into how I approach documentation and repeatable fixes in IT. If I solve something once and it's likely to happen again, I don't just fix it, I write down how, because the version of me, or my colleague, dealing with it next month shouldn't have to solve it from scratch.

## Leadership looked different than I expected

Leadership was the other big one, and it wasn't what I expected it to look like once I got here. In the Army, leadership at my level was mostly about making sure the people under you had what they needed to do their job properly, and taking responsibility when something went wrong rather than passing it down. I didn't walk into an MSP as a manager, I walked in at the bottom of the technical ladder, which meant putting that instinct on hold for a while and focusing on being good at the actual technical work first. But it's part of why I'm drawn toward service governance and process improvement roles now rather than staying purely technical. I like making the system work better for the people using it, not just fixing the individual fault in front of me.

## The transition wasn't smooth

The transition itself wasn't smooth, to be honest. Civilian job applications don't come with a rank structure or a clear next step, and the first few months of translating "led a team through X" into language a recruiter would recognise was genuinely difficult. Certifications helped with that. ITIL gave me a shared vocabulary with people who'd come up through a completely different route, and it meant I wasn't just relying on "trust me, I can lead" without anything to back it up on paper.

## What actually transfers

If you're reading this from the other side of a similar transition, whether that's forces to civilian or just switching industries entirely, the thing I'd say is that the skills usually transfer more directly than the job titles do. Nobody's going to hire you because you can navigate a rank structure. They might hire you because you can be handed an ambiguous problem, work out a repeatable way to solve it, document it properly, and make sure the next person doesn't have to start from zero. That's the same job, just in a different uniform.
