I get asked fairly often how someone goes from 13 years in the British Army into IT.

Usually with the assumption that the two have nothing in common.

I get why it looks that way from the outside. But a lot of what I do day to day traces back to things I learned in uniform, long before I touched a service desk.

The obvious answer people expect is discipline. Sure, that's in there. But it's not the interesting bit.

The interesting bit is process.

The Army runs on process because the alternative, in a lot of situations, is genuinely dangerous. Every task has a standard way of being done. That standard exists because someone worked out the safest, most reliable way to do it. You don't skip steps because you're in a hurry.

That builds a habit. Is there a better, more consistent way to do this. Rather than just doing the thing in front of you and moving on.

That habit transferred almost directly into how I approach documentation and repeatable fixes. If I solve something once and it's likely to happen again, I write down how. Because the version of me dealing with it next month shouldn't have to solve it from scratch.

Leadership was the other big one. And it didn't look like what I expected once I got here.

In the Army, leadership at my level was mostly about making sure the people under you had what they needed to do their job properly. And taking responsibility when something went wrong, rather than passing it down.

I didn't walk into an MSP as a manager. I walked in as L1 support. That meant putting that instinct on hold for a while and focusing on being good at the actual technical work first.

But it's part of why I care more about fixing the thing that keeps causing the fault, not just the fault itself.

The transition wasn't smooth, if I'm honest. Civilian job applications don't come with a rank structure or a clear next step. The first few months of translating led a team through X into language a recruiter would recognise was genuinely difficult.

Professional certifications helped with that. They gave me a shared vocabulary with people who'd come up through a completely different route. I wasn't just relying on trust me, I can lead, with nothing to back it up.

If you're on the other side of a similar transition, forces to civilian or otherwise, here's what I'd say.

The skills usually transfer more directly than the job titles do.

Nobody's going to hire you because you can navigate a rank structure. They might hire you because you can be handed an ambiguous problem, work out a repeatable way to solve it, write it down properly, and make sure the next person doesn't start from zero.

That's the same job. Just a different uniform.
