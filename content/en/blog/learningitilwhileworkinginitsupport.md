One of the more interesting parts of studying ITIL while working in IT support is recognising things you've already experienced.

You read about an idea and think:

> I've seen that.

Not because you've been following a textbook.

Because you've already dealt with something similar in the real world.

## The theory gives you another way to look at the work

Before studying ITIL, I already understood that recurring incidents were annoying.

I already knew that documentation mattered.

I already knew that a bad escalation could waste time.

I already knew that some problems needed to be looked at beyond the individual ticket.

What ITIL gave me was a way to structure those observations.

That has been useful.

## Incident management made sense quickly

The basic idea of restoring service and understanding the impact is very familiar in support.

Someone can't work.

Something isn't available.

Something has stopped behaving as expected.

The immediate job is to get things moving again.

But working in support also shows you that incidents don't all have the same impact.

One person having an issue isn't necessarily the same as a whole team being unable to work.

Understanding that context helps with prioritisation.

That's something I already do in practice, but studying the concepts has made me more deliberate about it.

## Then there is what happens afterwards

This is where my thinking has changed more.

If the same thing happens repeatedly, I don't just want to keep solving the same ticket.

I want to know whether there is something behind it.

That might be a recurring technical fault.

It might be documentation.

It might be a process.

It might be something another team owns.

The important thing is noticing the pattern.

## Value streams were probably the biggest eye-opener

The idea of looking at how work flows from demand to an outcome is something I found particularly interesting.

Because that is what happens every day in support.

A user needs something.

A request or incident enters the service.

Someone triages it.

Someone investigates it.

It might move between teams.

Eventually, the user gets an outcome.

There are plenty of opportunities for things to slow down along the way.

A poor handover.

Missing information.

An approval.

A queue.

Unclear ownership.

An outdated process.

Once you start thinking about the flow rather than just your part of it, those things become easier to notice.

## The interesting bit is applying it without forcing it

I don't want to start describing every ticket in ITIL terminology.

That would get annoying very quickly.

Instead, I find it more useful to ask better questions.

What outcome are we trying to achieve?

What is the actual impact?

Where is this work getting stuck?

Is this a repeat issue?

Does the documentation help?

Is there a better way to do this?

Those questions are useful whether you call them ITIL questions or not.

## Learning something and seeing it at work are different

I've always found that I understand something better once I've seen it in practice.

Reading about a concept is one thing.

Then you see a real ticket and suddenly it makes sense.

That's one of the reasons I've enjoyed the ITIL courses.

I've been able to connect the material back to things I've actually dealt with.

And sometimes the opposite happens.

Something I've seen at work makes me ask a much better question during the course.

## I'm still building the experience

There is a difference between understanding a framework and having years of formal service-management experience.

I'm not claiming the latter.

I'm building that experience through the work I'm already doing and using the study to give me a better understanding of what I'm seeing.

That's the part I find useful.

The course gives me the concepts.

The job gives me the examples.

The combination makes the learning stick.

## That's probably why I like ITIL

It doesn't replace practical experience.

For me, it gives the practical experience another layer of structure.

And the more I learn, the more I find myself asking questions that go beyond:

> How do I fix this ticket?

Sometimes the better question is:

> What is happening around this ticket?

That's a useful question to have.
