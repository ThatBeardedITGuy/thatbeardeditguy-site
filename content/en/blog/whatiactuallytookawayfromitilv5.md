I've completed ITIL v5 Foundation, Strategy and Product.

I'm not interested in turning every conversation about IT into an ITIL lesson, and I definitely don't think a certificate suddenly makes someone an expert.

For me, the useful part has been having a framework that gives structure to things I've already seen in support.

## Value is bigger than activity

One of the ideas I've found useful is thinking about value and outcomes rather than just the activity being performed.

In support, it's easy to think:

> Ticket resolved.

Job done.

But what did that actually achieve?

Did the user get back to work?

Was the issue resolved properly?

Did we understand what happened?

Did we leave useful information behind?

Did anything need to change afterwards?

The ticket is an activity.

The outcome is what matters to the person receiving the service.

## The four dimensions made sense to me

The four dimensions are another part of ITIL that I found useful because they stop you looking at a service through only one lens.

People and organisations matter.

Information and technology matter.

Partners and suppliers matter.

Value streams and processes matter.

I've seen examples of all four in normal support work.

You can have the right technology and still have a poor process.

You can have a good process and still have unclear ownership.

You can have good people and poor information.

Usually the service is a combination of all of these things.

## Value streams changed how I look at tickets

I found the idea of looking at how work actually flows from demand to an outcome particularly interesting.

A ticket doesn't just exist in isolation.

Someone raises demand.

Someone triages it.

Someone investigates.

Maybe another team gets involved.

There might be approvals.

There might be changes.

The user gets an outcome.

The work moves through different people and systems.

Once you start looking at that flow, you can start noticing where work gets stuck.

That is something I find genuinely interesting.

## Continual improvement isn't just a big project

Another thing I took from ITIL is that improvement doesn't always have to mean a huge transformation project.

Sometimes it's a small change.

Update a document.

Remove an unnecessary step.

Make an escalation clearer.

Spot a recurring issue.

Improve a handover.

Make information easier to find.

Those changes can add up.

I've already seen examples of this in my own work.

## Strategy isn't just something for senior management

The Strategy course also made me think more about the relationship between what an organisation wants to achieve and the services that support it.

That sounds like something that belongs far away from a service desk.

But support still affects whether services work for people.

If users can't access a system, if a process is unreliable or if recurring problems are consuming time, that has an effect somewhere else in the organisation.

I don't need to be setting corporate strategy to understand that connection.

## Product made me think about the whole thing

The Product course built on some of those ideas for me.

I found myself thinking more about products and services as things that develop over time rather than something that gets built once and forgotten.

Requirements change.

Technology changes.

User needs change.

The environment changes.

That means the service needs to be looked at as a whole.

## The biggest takeaway

Probably the biggest thing I've taken from ITIL is that it has given names and structure to things I was already starting to notice.

Why does the same issue keep happening?

Where does work get stuck?

Is the documentation helping?

Who actually needs to be involved?

What outcome are we trying to achieve?

What could we improve?

Those are useful questions whether you know the ITIL terminology or not.

## I'm still learning

There is plenty of ITIL I haven't applied in a formal service-management role.

I'm not going to pretend the opposite.

What I can say is that studying it has changed the way I look at the work I already do.

The framework sits in the background.

The practical experience is still the thing in front of me.

For me, that's the useful combination.
