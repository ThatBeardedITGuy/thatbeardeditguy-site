For a while, documentation was something I did after the fact. If I did it at all.

Fix the thing. Close the ticket. Move on. It felt like writing it up was slowing me down when the actual point was getting through the queue.

What changed my mind wasn't a training course. It was covering for a colleague who was off sick for two weeks.

A ticket came in referencing a known workaround for a client's SharePoint permissions issue. Except there was no note anywhere explaining what that workaround actually was.

I spent the best part of an hour rediscovering something someone else had already solved. Purely because it lived in their head and not in the ticket system.

That's an hour that shouldn't have existed.

And it's not really about that one ticket. It's about how often that hour repeats itself across a team when nobody writes anything down.

Every undocumented fix is a tax that gets paid again by whoever hits the same issue next. Whether that's a colleague, or future you, having forgotten the details.

So a few things changed.

I write the root cause in the ticket notes now, not just the fix. Even when the fix is a thirty second job. Future me needs the why, not just the what.

If it's the second time I've seen something, it gets a proper write-up somewhere searchable. Not just another quick ticket closure.

I try to write as if a colleague with less context than me is going to read it next week. Not as if I'm reminding myself of something I already understand.

And screenshots, exact error text, that goes in. Not a paraphrased summary. It didn't work tells nobody anything six months from now.

Honestly, it slowed me down slightly on individual tickets. And sped the team up considerably over time.

That trade is easy to underrate when you're measured on how fast you close things today.

Much easier to see once you've been on the other end of someone else's undocumented fix.
