Most IT support can be done without physically being in the same place as the user.

Logs are available remotely.

Devices can be managed remotely.

Users can share screenshots.

Teams calls can solve a lot of problems.

But being on-site changes things.

## You see the environment

A ticket gives you a description of a problem.

Being on-site gives you the environment around it.

You can see how people are using the equipment.

You can see where devices are located.

You can notice physical issues.

You can see whether something that sounded like an isolated problem is actually affecting a wider area.

Sometimes that context makes the problem much easier to understand.

## The conversation is different

Users explain things differently when you're standing next to them.

That's not a criticism.

It's just easier to demonstrate a problem than describe it.

Someone might say:

> Outlook isn't working.

Then you sit down with them and discover the actual issue is a shared mailbox, a particular calendar permission or something else entirely.

Being there gives you the opportunity to ask questions while the user shows you what they mean.

## You're also responsible for the wider visit

When I'm the sole engineer on-site, I'm not just dealing with whatever ticket happens to be in front of me.

I'm responsible for coordinating the support activity during the visit.

That can mean triaging issues, deciding what needs attention first, supporting users, gathering information and making sure anything that can't be resolved on-site gets passed to the right team.

You have to keep the bigger picture in mind.

## You don't always have another engineer beside you

Being the sole engineer on-site means being comfortable making decisions.

Not necessarily big decisions.

Sometimes it's simply:

> This needs investigating further.

Or:

> This needs to go to the infrastructure team.

Or:

> We can resolve this now.

The important part is knowing what you can deal with yourself and when you need to involve someone else.

That comes back to ownership.

If I can't fix something on-site, I still want to make sure the investigation and handover are useful.

## Site visits also show the difference between process and reality

This is probably the bit I've found most interesting.

A process can look straightforward on paper.

Then you actually have to use it with a real person, in a real environment, with whatever constraints happen to exist that day.

That's where you find the gaps.

Maybe a step is missing.

Maybe the documentation is outdated.

Maybe the user has been doing something differently for years.

Maybe the process works, but nobody knows where the information is.

Those observations can be useful.

They don't necessarily mean the process is bad.

They might just mean it needs to be clearer.

## The Windows 11 migration

One of the bigger pieces of on-site work I've supported was a Windows 11 migration involving more than 200 users.

My role included readiness checks, floor-walking, triage and escalation while acting as the on-site support lead.

That kind of work is a good example of why technical support and service delivery overlap.

You're dealing with devices and software, but you're also dealing with people, timing, communication and the impact of problems on their ability to work.

The technical fix is only part of the job.

## What I've learned from being on-site

Being on-site has made me a better support engineer.

Not because I suddenly know more commands.

Because I get to see the service from the user's side.

I can see the environment.

I can hear how people describe the problem.

I can spot things that aren't obvious from a ticket.

And I can take responsibility for making sure the visit actually achieves what it needs to.

Remote support is incredibly useful.

But sometimes being there makes all the difference.
