There's a well worn path in support work. Get good at the technical fixes. Take on harder faults. Specialise deeper over time.

Sensible path. Works for a lot of people.

What I've noticed about myself is that it's not the part of the job that actually pulls my attention.

When I look back at what I've genuinely enjoyed most, it isn't the satisfaction of solving a gnarly technical fault. Though that has its moments.

It's the stuff around the fault.

Noticing a ticket is a repeat. Working out why a process keeps producing the same failure. Writing something down so it doesn't happen again. Building a slightly better way of doing a recurring task.

That's service management and process improvement territory, not pure technical depth.

I'm more curious about why something keeps happening than about the cleverest way to fix it this one time.

None of this is a rejection of technical work. Being competent at the fundamentals is what makes the process thinking useful in the first place. It's just a decision about which skill I want to keep sharpening.

A fault fixed well once is useful.

A process improved so the fault stops happening at all is useful to everyone who would have hit it next.

That second thing is closer to what I find myself gravitating toward. How I document a fix. How I read a pattern across a handful of tickets. How I think about the process behind a recurring issue, rather than just the instance of it in front of me.

If you're in support and finding yourself more interested in the pattern behind the tickets than the fault in front of you, that's worth paying attention to.

It doesn't have to mean changing what you do day to day.

It might just mean noticing where your actual curiosity sits.
